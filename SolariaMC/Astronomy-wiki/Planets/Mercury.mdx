# Mercury: The Innermost World

*Composition, structure, surface ages, and temperature*

**What you will learn:**
- Where Mercury sits and what kind of planet it is
- Why Mercury has so much metal
- How Mercury got a layered interior
- How scientists use craters to date surfaces
- Why Mercury is not the hottest planet

---

## 1. Meet the Planet

- Mercury is the **1st planet** from the Sun, the **innermost** world.
- It is the **smallest planet** in the solar system.
- It is a **terrestrial planet** (also called an *inner* or *rocky* planet), like Venus, Earth, and Mars. It has a **solid surface**, unlike the gas and ice giants of the outer solar system.
- Its structure is dominated by **heavy elements** (rock and metal) instead of the hydrogen and helium that make up giant planets.

| Feature | Mercury |
|---|---|
| **Position** | 1st planet from the Sun |
| **Category** | Terrestrial / inner planet |
| **Size** | Smallest planet |
| **Surface** | Solid, rocky and metallic |
| **Sunlit surface temperature** | 280 °C to 430 °C |
| **Main materials** | Metals (mostly iron) and silicate rocks |

## 2. Density, Composition, and Interior

### Oxidized Chemistry

Terrestrial planets are **oxidized**, dominated by **oxygen compounds** and heavy elements that are rarer in the universe as a whole.

- **Metals:** The most common metal in terrestrial planets is **iron**.
- **Rocks:** The most abundant rocks are **silicates** (silicon + oxygen).

### Mercury Is Extra Metallic

| Body | Metal share of mass |
|---|---|
| Earth, Venus, Mars | About **1/3** (iron-nickel or iron-sulfur), with about 2/3 silicates |
| **Mercury** | **Highest proportion of metals** of any terrestrial body |
| Earth's Moon | **Lowest** proportion of metals |

We know this because of Mercury's **high density**. A planet with more metal is heavier for its size.

### Differentiation: How Mercury Got Layers

**Differentiation** is gravity sorting a molten planet into layers by density.

1. **Heat it up.** The planet had to be heated to the melting point of rock, typically **above 1300 K**.
2. **Metal sinks.** Dense **iron** and other metals sank to form the **core**.
3. **Silicates float.** Lighter silicates rose to form the **mantle and crust**.
4. **Cool and freeze.** The layers locked in as the planet cooled and solidified.

### Diagram: Mercury's Layers

```
  SPACE
  ==============================================
  |  CRUST                                      |  LIGHTEST
  |  light silicate minerals                    |     ^
  |  floated up while molten                    |     |  rises
  |---------------------------------------------|     |
  |  MANTLE                                     |  MEDIUM
  |  silicate minerals                          |     |
  |---------------------------------------------|     |  sinks
  |  CORE                                       |     |
  |  dense iron and other metals                |     v
  |  sank to the center                         |  DENSEST
  ==============================================
  CENTER OF MERCURY
```

> **Fun fact:** Mercury's core takes up an unusually large share of the planet, because Mercury has the most metal of any terrestrial body.

## 3. Reading a Surface: Crater Counting

Mercury is hard, solid, and covered in impact marks. It has no thick atmosphere, so craters stay put. Scientists use **crater counting** to estimate surface age.

### The Logic

1. **Constant impact rate:** Space rocks have hit planets at a **roughly constant rate** for several billion years.
2. **Proportional:** If nothing erases craters, the **number of craters per area** is **proportional to how long that surface has been exposed**.
3. **Resurfacing resets the clock:** Big events like **lava flows** or **impact ejecta** can "sweep clean" a region. Crater counts show how long it has been since the last reset.
4. **Older = more craters.** Undisturbed old terrain has far more craters than younger terrain.

| Surface | Crater density | Age |
|---|---|---|
| Never resurfaced | High | Old |
| Recently resurfaced | Low | Young |

## 4. Heat and Sunlight

### How Hot Is Mercury?

On its **sunlit side**, Mercury's surface reaches **280 °C to 430 °C** (about **553 K to 703 K**).

### How Distance Changes Temperature

Sunlight energy **weakens with the square of the distance** from the Sun. As a result, surface temperature falls **roughly with the square root of the distance**.

```
  Temperature drop  ~=  square root of (how much farther away)
```

**Example: Mercury vs. Pluto**
- Pluto's closest approach to the Sun is about **30 AU**, roughly **100 times** Mercury's distance (per the course notes).
- Temperature drops by about **square root of 100 = 10**.
- A sunlit baseline of about **500 K** drops to about **50 K**.

### Proximity Isn't Everything

| Planet | Distance | Atmosphere | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mercury** | Closest to the Sun | Almost none, so no insulation | Hot by day, but **not** the hottest |
| **Venus** | Farther out | Dense CO₂ **blanket** (greenhouse effect) | **Hotter than Mercury** |

> **Big idea:** Atmosphere can matter more than distance for surface temperature.

## 5. The Terrestrial Worlds Compared

| Body | Position | Surface | Metal share | Main thermal driver |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Mercury** | 1st | Solid, heavily cratered | **Highest** | Direct sunlight, extreme sunlit heating (280 to 430 °C) |
| **Venus** | 2nd | Solid | Standard (about 1/3) | Massive CO₂ insulation, hotter than Mercury |
| **Earth** | 3rd | Solid | Standard (about 1/3) | Moderate greenhouse insulation, keeps liquid water |
| **Mars** | 4th | Solid | Standard (about 1/3) | Thin atmosphere, weak sunlight because of distance |
| **Moon** | Earth's satellite | Solid | **Lowest** | Uninsulated solar surface radiation |

## Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Terrestrial planet** | Rocky planet with a solid surface |
| **Oxidized chemistry** | Dominated by oxygen compounds like silicates |
| **Silicate** | Mineral made of silicon and oxygen |
| **Differentiation** | Sorting of a molten planet into layers by density |
| **Crater counting** | Estimating surface age from the number of impact craters |
| **Resurfacing** | Lava or ejecta covering old terrain and erasing craters |
| **Greenhouse effect** | Atmosphere trapping heat like a blanket |
| **AU** | Astronomical unit, the average Earth-Sun distance |

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

2. It has the highest proportion of metals (mainly iron) of any terrestrial body.
3. It had to be heated above rock's melting point (over 1300 K) so heavy metals could sink to form a core while lighter silicates floated up.
4. By counting impact craters per area. Impact rates have been roughly constant, so more craters mean an older surface that hasn't been erased by lava flows or ejecta.
5. Venus has a dense carbon dioxide atmosphere that traps heat (greenhouse effect). Mercury has no such insulation.
