# Mercury: The Innermost Planet

*Composition, surface, and place in the solar system*

**What you will learn:**
- Where Mercury sits in the solar system and what makes it extreme
- What Mercury's surface looks like and why it is so heavily cratered
- How Mercury compares to Earth in size, temperature, and atmosphere
- Why Mercury has almost no atmosphere despite being closest to the Sun

---

## 1. Meet Mercury

- Mercury is the **smallest planet** in the solar system and the **closest to the Sun**.
- A year on Mercury lasts only **88 Earth days**, but a day (one full rotation) takes about **59 Earth days** — almost two-thirds of its year.
- Because it rotates so slowly and has virtually no atmosphere, temperatures swing wildly: up to **430 °C** in the day and down to **−180 °C** at night.

| Feature | Mercury |
|---|---|
| **Type** | Terrestrial (rocky) planet |
| **Distance from Sun** | About 0.39 AU |
| **Surface** | Grey silicate rock, volcanic plains, dense impact craters |
| **Atmosphere** | Almost none (extremely thin exosphere) |
| **Moons** | None |

---

## 2. Surface and Terrain

Mercury's surface is the most heavily cratered of any planet, because:
- It has **no substantial atmosphere** to burn up incoming meteoroids.
- It has had **no plate tectonics** to erase old craters.
- Its surface has changed very little since the end of the Late Heavy Bombardment (~3.8 billion years ago).

### Key surface features

- **Impact craters** — ranging from tiny pits to enormous basins (e.g. Caloris Basin, ~1,550 km wide).
- **Smooth volcanic plains** — dark lowland regions where ancient lava once flooded crater floors.
- **Scarps (rupes)** — long cliffs formed as Mercury's interior cooled and shrank, wrinkling the crust.

---

## 3. Mercury's Extreme Temperatures

Despite being closest to the Sun, Mercury is **not** the hottest planet — Venus is. Mercury's lack of atmosphere means:
- **No greenhouse effect** to trap heat.
- **No thermal blanket** at night — heat radiates away rapidly.

This produces the largest day-to-night temperature range of any planet in the solar system.

## Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Terrestrial planet** | A rocky, Earth-like planet (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars) |
| **Exosphere** | An extremely tenuous outer layer of gas, not dense enough to be called a true atmosphere |
| **Caloris Basin** | One of the largest known impact craters in the solar system, on Mercury |
| **Scarp (rupes)** | A cliff formed by crustal compression as a planet's interior cools and shrinks |
| **Late Heavy Bombardment** | A period ~3.8–4.1 billion years ago of intense asteroid and comet impacts |

## Review Questions

1. Why does Mercury have such extreme temperature swings between day and night?
2. Why is Mercury the most heavily cratered planet?
3. Mercury is the closest planet to the Sun — why is Venus hotter?
4. What is a scarp (rupes) and what causes them on Mercury?

### Answers

1. Mercury's near-total lack of atmosphere means there is nothing to trap heat at night or distribute it from the dayside. Temperatures swing from +430 °C in daylight to −180 °C in darkness.
2. Mercury has no substantial atmosphere to vaporise incoming meteoroids and no active geology to erase old craters. Its surface has been geologically inactive for billions of years.
3. Venus has a thick carbon dioxide atmosphere that creates an intense greenhouse effect, trapping far more heat than Mercury retains. Mercury's lack of atmosphere means it cannot hold heat.
4. Scarps are long cliffs formed when Mercury's interior cooled and contracted after formation. The shrinking core caused the crust to buckle and compress, pushing rock upward into steep ridges.
